Pickleball Dink Strategy: How to Trap Opponents & Win the Net

So I'm watching this dink rally last month at a 4.5 tournament in Mesa. Twenty-three shots—I counted because I'm that guy apparently. And both players look great. Soft hands, good footwork, all of it. But here's the thing that got me: only one of them actually had a plan. The other one? Just trying not to lose. Big difference.

If you've ever been stuck at the kitchen trading dinks thinking "when does this END already"—yeah, you're asking the wrong question. Wrong question entirely. What you should be asking is how do I get this person to mess up before I do?

Because someone's gonna mess up. Always happens. Might as well be them.

Most people think dinking is defensive. Like you're just keeping the ball in play, waiting for something to happen. That's not it at all. Not even close. When you get to 4.0, maybe 4.5, the dink becomes this whole other thing. It's how you control the point. How you make people uncomfortable. How you get them to finally crack and do something dumb.

And look—I'm not gonna sit here and give you "7 tips for better dinks" or whatever. You've probably read ten of those already. Hasn't helped much, right? What I'm gonna do instead is show you the actual traps. The stuff I've seen work at PPA events, things I teach at my clinics out in Scottsdale, strategies that guys like Ben Johns use when there's actual money on the line.

Why Dinks Matter More Than You Think

Okay so. I play Tuesday nights at this rec center up in North Phoenix. Good group of people—mostly 3.5s and 4.0s, couple guys who used to play tennis, there's this one woman Karen who played racquetball in college and honestly her hands are ridiculous. Anyway.

Every week I see the same exact thing happen.

Somebody gets into a dink rally. Goes maybe eight, nine, ten shots. Then you can literally watch them get impatient. They see a ball that's like... maybe attackable? Kinda? And they go for it. Sails long. Or dumps in the net. Or pops up so high the other person just destroys it.

Point over. Happens constantly.

Here's what's actually going on when you're in a dink battle:

First—you're making them play slow. Fast players absolutely hate this. They want pace. You're not giving them pace. Already messing with their head.

Second—if your dink is low enough, they have to hit up on it. Like there's no other option. Physics. They cannot attack a ball that's below the net unless they want to hit it into the net. Which, I mean, some people do. But that's their problem.

Third—patience runs out. For most rec players? Probably 12, maybe 15 seconds of dinking before that voice in their head goes "do SOMETHING." And then they do something. Usually something bad.

"The people who beat me aren't hitting harder than me. They're just... they're fine being in that uncomfortable spot way longer than I am. I crack first. That's usually why I lose." — Guy I coached last summer, after we finally figured out his problem

Want a depressing stat? I tracked 47 dink rallies at a tournament in Gilbert last October. Just sat there with my phone counting. 31 of them ended on unforced errors. That's 66%. Not winners—errors. People attacking balls they had no business attacking.

The other 16 ended on actual good plays. Setups, traps, forced mistakes. Which is what we're getting into now.

Oh—but real quick, if you can't even get to the kitchen without getting picked off, you might wanna read our transition zone thing first. Can't win the dink battle if you never make it there.

5 Traps That Actually Work

Alright here we go. The good stuff.

None of this is theory by the way. I've seen all of these work in real games, taught all of them to students from like 3.5 up to 5.0. They work because they're based on how people actually behave. Not how they should behave in some perfect world.

Trap #1: Boring Crosscourt... Then Knife Them

Crosscourt dink is the safe play. Everyone knows this. Low part of the net, long diagonal, tons of room for error. Your opponent knows it too. They're expecting crosscourt.

That's exactly what you want.

Hit like five or six crosscourt dinks. Same spot every time. Boring. Repetitive. You want them to get comfortable. You want their feet to stop moving because they "know" where the ball's going next.

Then don't hit crosscourt.

Watched Anna Leigh Waters do this at the 2024 PPA Championships in Dallas. I was there, sitting maybe 20 rows back. She hit seven crosscourts in a row—I counted because again, I'm that guy. Seventh shot, her opponent was already cheating left. Weight shifting before the ball even left Waters' paddle.

Waters went middle. Soft little redirect, nothing special. But the other woman was just... wrong-footed. Half a step late. Ball popped up. Point over.

Whole sequence took maybe four seconds. But she set it up for 15 seconds before that with "boring" dinks.

Thing to remember: Being predictable isn't bad if you're the one who decides when to stop being predictable. Control the pattern, then break it.

Trap #2: Feet Feet Feet... Then Lob

When you dink at someone's feet—like right at their shoes—they gotta bend down. Reach. And when they do that their weight goes forward. Once your weight's forward you can't go backward fast. Just how bodies work.

So you dink at their feet three, four times in a row. Not the kitchen line—their actual shoes. Watch how they're standing. When you see them kinda hunched over, leaning forward waiting for another low one...

Lob.

This guy Dave taught me this. Older coach out of San Diego, maybe early 60s. Played tennis forever before pickleball. He said something that stuck with me—

"Lob's not cheap. You gotta earn it. You dink at their feet until they're leaning in, THEN the lob is free money. Do it backwards and you'll get burned every time." — Dave. Has definitely beaten people half his age more times than he can count.

I see random lobs all the time. No setup, no pattern, just "maybe this works?" It doesn't. But a lob after you've hit their feet four times? Works probably 70% of the time from what I've seen.

Oh and when someone does this TO you—yeah you're gonna want to know the reset shot. Trust me.

Trap #3: Hit the Marriage, Not the Players

In doubles there's this spot nobody owns. Right down the middle. And when you put a ball there, beautiful things happen. Both people hesitate. "Yours!" "Mine!" "I got—" By the time they figure it out they're late.

You're not trying to hit winners here. You're trying to create confusion. Aim for the spot where if both of them reached for it, their paddles would literally clunk into each other. Doesn't have to be perfect. Just close.

I got to ask Riley Newman a question at a pro-am thing last year. Somebody asked what beats rec players and he actually laughed.

"Middle. Just hit it down the middle. Most rec teams, they don't communicate at all. Put one ball in the middle and suddenly they're looking at each other instead of watching the ball. Do it twice and they'll start arguing." — Riley Newman. He wasn't kidding either.

I've literally seen couples fight over middle balls. Like actual tension. Okay maybe that's a slight exaggeration but... not much of one.

Point is you're not attacking the court. You're attacking the communication between two people. Even if they get it back clean, you've planted a seed. Now they're thinking about who takes what instead of thinking about the point.

Mix middle balls in randomly. Don't do three in a row—too obvious. Just one every four or five shots. Keeps them guessing.

Trap #4: The Waiting Game (aka Mental Torture)

Most players have like an internal clock. After 10 maybe 12 dinks something in their brain starts yelling "DO SOMETHING." You can actually see it happen. They get twitchy. Fidgety.

That little voice? Your new best friend.

This one isn't about a specific shot. It's mindset. Stay calm. Hit the same boring dink over and over. Don't try to win. Don't be clever. Just... keep... dinking.

And wait.

Did this experiment at my clinic in Scottsdale last March. Eight 4.0 players, mostly retired engineer types who overthink literally everything (love em though). Split em into two groups.

Group A: "Be aggressive. Look for speedups. Try to end points fast."

Group B: "Keep everything in the kitchen. No attacks at all. Just outlast them."

20 points. Group B won 14.

And here's what killed me—every point Group A won was a legitimate winner. Great shot, nothing you can do. But every point they LOST? Unforced errors. Attacks they didn't need to make. One guy sped up a ball that was below his knees. Below his knees! Why? Because he was sick of dinking.

Hard truth: If you're tired of dinking after 15 shots, good. Your opponent's tired too. Difference is you KNOW it's supposed to feel like this. They don't. Keep going.

Trap #5: The Invisible String Thing

Best doubles teams look like they're connected somehow. One shifts left, other shifts left. One poaches, other covers. It's weird how synced up they are. And it makes everything harder for opponents because there's never an open court.

Simple version: one partner handles crosscourt, other one looks to poach middle. When you poach your partner slides behind you. That's basically it. Practice til it's automatic.

Mental image that works for most people—pretend there's like a 6-foot string connecting your paddle to your partner's. They move, you move. String can stretch a bit, you're not robots. But shouldn't go slack. Shouldn't snap.

Played against this husband-wife team in Chandler last summer. They clearly drilled this. It was so annoying. Every time I thought I found an opening somebody was already there. Middle? Covered. Line? Covered. I hit this shot I was sure was a winner and the wife just... materialized. Like she knew where I was going before I did.

They beat us 11-4. Didn't even look tired.

Real Quick—Check Your Mechanics

Before anything else, gotta be honest about something.

None of these traps matter if you can't dink consistently. Can't build a pattern if every third ball goes in the net. Can't play mental games when you're the one messing up.

So real talk. Can you hit 20 dinks in a row without missing? Not winners—just 20 balls in the kitchen back and forth. If the answer's no, that's fine. But means you got homework before this other stuff will work.

Quick checklist:

  • Hit it out front. If you're making contact next to your hip you've already lost. Get that paddle forward where you can see it.
  • Grip soft. Move the shoulder. Hold the paddle like you're holding a bird or something—firm enough it won't fly away, soft enough you won't crush it. Weird image but it works. Power from shoulder, not wrist. Wrist creates chaos.
  • Stay low. You stand up straight at the kitchen line, I guarantee you're popping balls up. Knees bent. Weight forward. Balls of your feet. Stay athletic.

If this stuff feels shaky go check out our dink basics guide. No shame. We all started somewhere.

Mistakes I See Literally Every Week

I keep a running list in my head. Every clinic, every Tuesday night, every tournament—same stuff, different people. Almost comforting at this point.

  • Hitting too hard. Big one. Like 90% of dink errors. You try to add just a bit more pace, bit more depth, and suddenly the ball's flying into the back fence. If they can attack your dink, it's not a dink. It's a gift.
  • Crosscourt robot mode. Crosscourt's safe, yeah. But if you hit crosscourt 47 times with zero variation they're gonna figure it out. Ask me how I know.
  • Attacking stuff you shouldn't. Just because you can reach it doesn't mean you should speed it up. You're stretched out? Off balance? Ball's below the net? Not attackable. That's a reset. Live for another shot.
  • Feet like concrete. Every popup—every single one—comes from bad footwork. Player was too far back, didn't shuffle over, just stood there frozen. Your feet set up your hands, not the other way around.

Ran a clinic in Tempe last April and tracked every error for two hours. 43 errors total. 37 of them—86 percent—came from attacking too early. Not even bad attacks necessarily. Just attacks that didn't need to happen yet.

Fix is simple to understand, hard to actually do: trust your dink. Trust that if you keep it low and in the kitchen long enough, they'll screw up eventually. Patience beats impulse. Literally every time.

Drills Worth Your Time

So you know the traps now. Know the mistakes. But you gotta actually build these skills which means drilling. Thing is—I've watched people "practice" dinking for an hour and get nothing out of it because they weren't being intentional about it.

These actually help:

  • Pattern Breaker: Crosscourt rally with partner. Five dinks crosscourt, then redirect the sixth—middle or down the line. Partner does the same. You're not trying to win. You're trying to make that redirect look exactly like your crosscourt until the last second. Same body, same paddle angle, different result.
  • Feet to Lob: One person hits only at the opponent's feet. Four or five dinks, right at the shoes. Receiver stays in position—no backing up. After the fifth one, feeder throws a lob. See if they can recover. You learn fast what "leaning forward" looks like.
  • Chaos Middle: Regular doubles point but whoever hits one to the middle gets a bonus point. Just one per rally counts. Teaches you to find the gap. Also teaches you to close it when you're defending.
  • 50 Ball Grind: You and a partner. One rule—get to 50 dinks without missing. No attacks, no winners. Just 50 in a row. When you can do that, go for 75. Then 100. This builds the patience and muscle memory that makes everything else possible.

Key word is intentional. You're not just keeping the ball alive. You're practicing specific patterns, specific spots, specific decisions. Random reps build random skills. Intentional reps build weapons.

So Now What

Back to that rally I mentioned. 23 shots. Two solid players. One winner.

Winner's name was Diane, 4.5 out of Tempe. She didn't hit anything amazing. Nothing that'd make a highlight reel. She just kept asking the same question over and over: can you handle this? How bout this one? What about 22 in a row?

Eventually her opponent answered wrong.

That's what dinking actually is. Not defensive. Not passive. Patience turned into a weapon. Every soft shot is a test. Person who passes more tests wins.

"I don't dink to keep the ball alive. I dink to take control. By the time I speed up, I've already won the point. The attack's just a formality." — Ben Johns. Still think about this one way too much.

You got five traps now. Know the mechanics that matter. Know the mistakes to avoid and drills that'll actually help.

Rest is just doing it. Get to the kitchen. Build patterns. Wait people out.

Player who controls the dink controls the game. Simple as that.

See you out there.
